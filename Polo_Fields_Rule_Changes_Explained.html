Polo Fields Rule Changes, Explained

A plain-English guide for homeowners to the proposed 2026 Bylaws and Covenants, compared with the 1991 originals

Based on the July 2026 drafts prepared by the Association's attorney and the committee's summaries. Written from the homeowner's point of view. Not an official Association document and not legal advice.

Start here

Polo Fields has two rulebooks. The Covenants (legally called the "Declaration") are the rules attached to your property. They say what you can build, how you can use your home, what you owe the Association, and what happens if you don't pay. The Bylaws are the rules for how the Association itself is run: meetings, votes, the Board, and its powers.

Both were written in 1991 for the developer. The Board's committee had an attorney (Wood + Lamping LLP) prepare amended and restated versions of both. If owners approve, the new versions replace the 1991 documents.

This guide explains what is changing and what it means for you. It starts with the biggest changes, then covers the changes that move control from owners to the Board, then goes topic by topic, and ends with a section-by-section list so you can follow along in the drafts.

One naming note: the 1991 documents call Board members "trustees." The drafts call them "directors." Same job.

Before anything else: the Handbook

Many of the changes in the new Covenants do not state a rule. They say the rule is in the Handbook. So the first thing to understand is what the Handbook is, who controls it, and what the new Covenants do with it.

Polo Fields already has a Handbook (the current file is labeled 2024). It is written by the Board and its Architectural Review Committee, not voted on by owners. It repeats the 1991 Covenants and adds the Board's own detailed rules: what fences may look like, where solar panels may go, what play structures may be built, how big a vegetable garden may be. The Handbook says the Board may fine you for breaking a Handbook rule. The 1991 Covenants contain no fine provision, and Ohio law allows a homeowners association to levy fines only "in accordance with the declaration" (R.C. 5312.11(A)(1)). The 2026 Covenants would add that provision (§4.4E).

Who can change it. The Handbook says its architectural standards "may be changed by a majority vote of the ARC [the Architectural Review Committee: the Board plus owners the Board appoints], subject to approval by the Board, or by the Board on its own accord," and that "any changes will be published to the Members." Owners do not vote on the Handbook, get no notice before a change, and have no way to overturn one. The 2026 drafts keep that arrangement: the Rules are "adopted by the Board of Directors as periodically updated and distributed" (Covenants §1.12), and the Secretary posts them on the website (Bylaws Art. IV §2.C). Neither draft adds notice, a comment period, an owner vote, or an owner override.

What the 2026 Covenants change. Today the Handbook's lot rules rest on the Board's power to approve plans under the 1991 Covenants (Art. VI). The 1991 Bylaws limit Board rules to "the use of the Common Areas and the personal conduct of the Members and their guests thereon" (Art. VII §1(a)), and Ohio's HOA law lets a board adopt rules for the common elements "and any other rules as the declaration provides" (R.C. 5312.06(D)(5)). The 2026 Covenants supply what the 1991 Covenants do not: they send the exterior appearance of your lot (§6.1), building materials (§6.2D), sign size and placement (§7.1E.1), décor limits (§7.1G.2), solar (§7.1I), accessory structures (§7.1K), lawn and landscaping (§7.1L), yard sales (§7.1N), and leasing (§7.1Q.J) to Handbook rules, and they authorize fines for violating "the Rules, Design Guidelines, or any provision of this Declaration" (§4.4E).

Rules only the owners can change become rules the Board writes. The 1991 Covenants set the fence style (three-rail split rail unless the Board approves otherwise, §6.2.11), the list of exterior materials (brick, stone, dryvit, with other materials by approval, §6.2.3), and the yard-sale limit (two a year, §7.1.9). Today the Board cannot change any of them without an owner vote; the Handbook restates the yard-sale limit and adds its own fence standards. Under the 2026 Covenants each becomes a Handbook rule the Board can rewrite on its own.

Handbook violations pick up the Covenants' remedies. Where a Covenant section adopts the Handbook's rules, breaking the rule is a violation of that section: a fine that becomes a lien on your home if unpaid for 10 days (§4.4E, §4.6), entry onto your lot on a Board vote to correct a violation (§9.1D), the Association's attorney fees "regardless of whether" a case is filed (§9.1), suspension of your vote and pool use while "in violation" (Bylaws Art. III §19.B.15), and, for tenants, eviction (§7.1Q.I). Ohio law gives you notice and a hearing before a fine (R.C. 5312.11(C)-(D)), and the draft gives 10 days' notice before an eviction; none of the others comes with a hearing.

Two places the draft takes back what the Handbook allows today. The Handbook says landscaping "does not require the Association's prior approval"; the draft requires approval for "substantially noticeable" landscaping changes (§6.1). The Handbook's satellite-dish rule requires no prior approval; the draft lists antennas and satellite dishes as modifications needing prior written approval (§6.1).

Bad for homeowners

The Board already changes the Handbook on its own and publishes the result. The 2026 Covenants make that Handbook the rulebook for your lot, move rules only the owners can change (fence style, materials, yard sales) into it, and attach liens, entry, attorney fees, and vote suspension to breaking it. No owner notice, comment, vote, or override, and no hearing beyond the fine hearing Ohio law requires.

How the vote works

The 1991 documents set the rules for this vote. The new Covenants take effect only if a written instrument is signed by at least 51% of all lot owners (1991 §9.3). The new Bylaws need 51% of all votes (1991 Bylaws Art. XI §6). In both cases that is a share of all lots, not of the people who happen to vote.

Once adopted, the new documents let future changes be made by ballot, by proxy (written permission for someone else to cast your vote), by mail, or electronically if the Board sets that up (Covenants §9.4; Bylaws Art. VII §5). Those methods do not govern this vote.

The Declaration draft notes that numbering and formatting will be cleaned up after final Board review, before it is sent to owners.

The changes that matter most

Ranked by how much control moves from owners to the Board, then by how much the change affects you.

  1. 1

    The Handbook becomes the binding rulebook for your lot

    Polo Fields already has a Handbook. The Board changes it without an owner vote and publishes the result; the drafts keep that process. What changes: the 2026 Covenants send the appearance of your lot, building materials, sign placement, décor limits, solar, accessory structures, lawn care, yard sales, and leasing to Handbook rules (§6.1, §6.2D, §7.1E.1, §7.1G.2, §7.1I, §7.1K, §7.1L, §7.1N, §7.1Q.J); rules only the owners can change today (fence style, exterior materials, two yard sales a year) become rules the Board can rewrite; and breaking a Handbook rule becomes a Covenant violation carrying a fine, a lien if unpaid, entry onto your lot, and attorney fees (§4.4E, §4.6, §9.1). The 1991 Bylaws limited Board rules to the common areas (Art. VII §1(a)), and the 1991 Covenants have no fine provision. The Handbook is not on the ballot.

    Bad for homeowners

    Rules the Board can change at will become binding on your lot, with liens, entry, and fees behind them, and no owner notice, comment, vote, or override in either draft.

  2. 2

    The Board can act on your property directly

    In 1991 the Association could already put a lien on your home for unpaid dues, foreclose it, and, after notice, do maintenance you failed to do and bill you (§5.1, §5.9, §8.4). The powers below are new. On a Board vote, the Association may enter your lot to remove or correct a "violation"; no notice is required (§9.1D). If the Board decides a pet is a nuisance, it may be "permanently removed" on 10 days' written notice (§7.1D). Decorations the Association "deems in its discretion" disturbing must come down (§7.1G.3). The Association may evict your tenant in your name and charge you the legal cost as a lien (§7.1Q.I). It may file a lien 10 days after a payment is late instead of 60, and may buy your home at its own foreclosure sale (§4.6). A fine, not just unpaid dues, becomes a lien. It may cut off your pool access on its own finding of damage or "threats" (§2.1C).

    Bad for homeowners

    Authority over your lot, your pet, your tenant, and your home itself, and none of it comes with a hearing. The entry power Ohio's HOA law itself gives a board is for "an imminent risk of damage or harm" (R.C. 5312.06(D)(13)); the draft's is broader.

  3. 3

    A new definition of "family" limits who can live in your home

    Today the Covenants say only "residential purposes." The draft adds a definition: a home may be occupied by no more than three people who are not related by blood, marriage, or legal adoption, and no home may be used as a "group home," "commercial foster home," boarding house, fraternity or sorority house, or "similar type of lodging, care, or treatment facility." This reaches ordinary households: two unmarried adults with two roommates; a family with more than three foster children, since foster children are not related by blood, marriage, or adoption; and a small group home for people with disabilities, which is banned outright. None of these terms is defined.

    Bad for homeowners

    Limits who can live in your home, and gives the Board the call on what counts as a "group home" or "commercial" foster home. As applied to group homes for people with disabilities and to foster families, it is the kind of rule federal fair-housing law addresses (City of Edmonds v. Oxford House, U.S. Supreme Court, 1995; 42 U.S.C. 3604(f), 3602(k)).

  4. 4

    The Board's powers over you as a member, with no hearing

    The Board could suspend your vote and pool use for any rule violation, with no hearing and no time limit (Bylaws Art. III §19.B.15); in 1991 that took a hearing and ended in 60 days. It could charge you the Association's attorney fees "regardless of whether" a lawsuit is filed (§9.1); 1991 had no such clause. It could change the recorded Covenants without an owner vote to resolve an "ambiguity" or clarify "original intent" (§9.4); in 1991 that power belonged to the developer. It could remove a director the owners elected for an alleged violation (Bylaws Art. III §5). The only hearing in either draft is for a director facing removal by the owners.

    Bad for homeowners

    Each removes a 1991 check or adds a power the owners never had to grant, and none comes with a hearing.

  5. 5

    Dues, fines, and liens

    The Board can still raise dues up to 10% a year on its own. The Board gains the power to charge a special assessment (a one-time charge on top of dues) for repairs and replacements without an owner vote; today every special assessment needs a two-thirds vote. New fines and individual charges can be added to your account. A lien can be placed on your home 10 days after a payment is late, with attorney and paralegal fees added.

    Bad for homeowners

    Removes the owner vote on the most common kind of special assessment and adds fines that attach to your home.

  6. 6

    The minimum turnout disappears, and non-resident owners can lose their vote

    Remote meetings, email notice, electronic voting and proxies. Any number of owners who show up is a quorum (the minimum needed to hold a meeting); today 15% of owners must be present. Owners who don't live in their home lose their vote if a lapse in their contact information keeps the Association from reaching them with required notices.

    Bad for homeowners

    A handful of owners can act for the whole neighborhood, and a non-resident owner's vote can be cancelled over a contact-information lapse.

  7. 7

    Almost any change to the outside of your home needs written approval

    Approval was already required in 1991 for buildings, fences, and exterior changes. Three things change. The list gets much longer and is "broadly construed": lighting, mailboxes, driveway resurfacing, noticeable landscaping changes, satellite dishes, solar panels, and more. The 1991 rule that a request is automatically approved if the Board does not answer within 30 days is gone. And the Covenants now point to the Handbook for the standards, which the Handbook already contains today. Nothing protects improvements you made legally under the 1991 rules.

    Bad for homeowners

    Removes the 30-day automatic approval, adds no deadline, reasons, or appeal in its place, and has no grandfather clause for existing improvements. For satellite dishes, the FCC rule (47 C.F.R. 1.4000) protects dishes one meter or less on property you control and bars rules that "unreasonably delay or prevent" installing one; the draft requires prior written approval for any dish.

  8. 8

    Violent felons and registered sex offenders could not live here

    Anyone convicted of a violent felony in any state or country, or registered as a Tier 2 or Tier 3 sex offender anywhere, could not occupy any lot, including a home they own. The ban has no time limit, "violent felony" is not defined, and the draft does not say whether it applies to people already living in Polo Fields. Landlords would need to screen tenants themselves; the Association says it has no duty to check.

  9. 9

    Signs expand; flags get a rule for the first time

    Signs: the 1991 rule allowed one for-sale sign and nothing else. The draft adds a for-rent sign, two security signs, two dog signs, and seasonal, holiday, and occasion signs. Political and religious signs stay banned, as they are today. Flags: the 1991 documents have no flag rule. The draft prohibits every flag except the U.S., Ohio, POW/MIA, and armed-services flags and seasonal, school, and sports-team flags, so political, cause, and religious flags become prohibited, and a flagpole needs approval. Decorations must be in "good taste" as the Association decides.

  10. 10

    Renting out your home would be banned, with exceptions

    Today there is no rental restriction. Under the new rules you could not rent your home unless the Board grants a 12-month "hardship" exception, decided at its sole discretion. Owners renting today keep the right until they sell, but only if they file paperwork within 30 days and are never 10 days late on dues. Short-term rentals (Airbnb, Vrbo) are banned for everyone.

    Bad for homeowners

    Takes away a use of your property you have today. The exception has no criteria, no appeal, and expires every 12 months.

  11. 11

    Pets, vehicles, trash, and yard sales

    Pets: 1991 allowed household pets with no conduct rules; the draft adds indoors at night, on a leash off your lot, and a removal power (see item 2). Vehicles: the 1991 limit was 48 hours a month for RVs, boats, and trailers; the draft covers more vehicles (golf carts, ATVs, campers, commercial vehicles, any trailer) but loosens the limit to 72 hours, and adds no parking in a yard. Trash: 1991 said out of view except pickup day; the draft requires the garage or an approved screen. Storage pods, not addressed in 1991, are banned unless inside the garage. Yard sales: the 1991 two-per-year limit moves to the Handbook.

  12. 12

    What gets easier or clearer

    Home-based businesses are allowed if they don't add traffic, employees, or noise. More kinds of signs are allowed (1991 allowed one for-sale sign only). Seasonal, holiday, school, and sports decorations are expressly permitted. Holiday lights can go up six weeks early instead of four. Sheds, pergolas, gazebos, and pool houses can be approved (sheds were banned outright). You can attend and vote remotely. Owners holding 25% of the votes can call a special meeting, down from 30%.

    Good for homeowners

    Each of these is a permission you do not have today.

Power shifts: changes that move control from owners to the Board

Each change below moves a decision from the owners to the Board, or removes a check that limits what the Board can do to an owner. Ranked by how much control moves. Under the top ten, "What you'll hear" is the answer the Board or committee is likely to give, and "What the documents say" is the reply from the 1991 text or Ohio law.

  1. 1

    Write binding rules for your own lot through the Handbook, with fines

    Covenants §1.13, §1.20, §4.4E, §6.1, §6.2D, §7.1E.1, §7.1G.2, §7.1I, §7.1K, §7.1L, §7.1N, §7.1Q.J; Bylaws Art. III §19.B.5, §19.B.10.c

    The Handbook exists today and the Board changes it on its own: its standards "may be changed by a majority vote of the ARC [Architectural Review Committee], subject to approval by the Board, or by the Board on its own accord," then "published to the Members." The 2026 Covenants make that Handbook the rulebook for fences, materials, lawn care, yard sales, solar, décor, signs, accessory structures, and leasing, move rules only the owners can change into it, and authorize fines for breaking it (§4.4E), with liens, entry, and attorney fees behind the fines. The drafts require only that a rule be distributed to owners and posted after adoption (§1.12; Bylaws Art. IV §2.C). No advance notice, no comment period, no owner vote, no owner override.

    What you'll hear

    "The Handbook has existed for years, the Board has always been able to update it, and Ohio law already lets us fine for rule violations."

    What the documents say

    The Handbook exists, and the Board updates it; that is the point. Under the 1991 documents the Board's rule-making power is limited to the common areas (Bylaws Art. VII §1(a)), the 1991 Declaration has no fine provision, and Ohio law allows other Board rules only "as the declaration provides" (R.C. 5312.06(D)(5)) and fines "imposed or levied in accordance with the declaration" (R.C. 5312.11(A)(1)). The 2026 Covenants supply both. Voting yes turns a document the Board rewrites at will into the binding rulebook for your lot.

  2. 2

    Suspend any owner's vote for a "violation"

    Bylaws Art. III §19.B.15 (1991 Art. VII §1(b))

    The Board alone decides who is "in violation," and that owner cannot vote, including on removing the Board. No hearing, no time limit. The 1991 Bylaws required notice and a hearing and capped it at 60 days. Ohio's HOA law (R.C. 5312.06(D)(15)) lists suspension only for being more than 30 days behind on dues.

    What you'll hear

    "This is the statute's own language; we would only suspend after the notice-and-hearing process for the underlying violation."

    What the documents say

    The statute's suspension power (R.C. 5312.06(D)(15)) covers unpaid dues only; "or who are in violation" is added by the draft. The hearing Ohio law requires (R.C. 5312.11) attaches to a fine, not to a suspension, and nothing in the draft ties a suspension to a completed hearing or gives it an end date. The 1991 Bylaws did both.

  3. 3

    Set the rules for, and count, its own election

    Bylaws Art. II §9, Art. III §3 (1991 Art. III §8, Art. IV §2, Art. V)

    The Board writes the nomination rules, the election procedure, and the voting-method rules. "Only persons nominated as candidates shall be eligible." The 1991 guarantees of nominations from the floor and owner-elected "inspectors of election" (an office the 1991 Bylaws list without describing) are gone, and the draft names no one to count votes.

    What you'll hear

    "The 1991 nominating committee was appointed and chaired by the Board too; secret ballot is an upgrade."

    What the documents say

    True, the 1991 committee was Board-chaired (Art. V). But 1991 also guaranteed two bypasses the draft removes: any owner could nominate from the floor (Art. V §1), and owners elected inspectors of election at each annual meeting (Art. III §8, item 6). Secret ballot is an improvement; it does not replace either safeguard.

  4. 4

    Charge special assessments for repairs, replacements, and "reasonable upgrades" without a vote

    Covenants §4.3A (1991 §5.4)

    Every special assessment needed a two-thirds owner vote in 1991. The draft lets the Board charge owners directly for repairing or replacing anything the Association already owns, including "reasonable upgrades," when the cost is unexpected and reserves are short. The Board decides what is unexpected.

    What you'll hear

    "The reserve requirement is the real protection, and a two-thirds vote at a low-turnout meeting let a few owners block urgent repairs."

    What the documents say

    The draft does require a reserve-funded budget (Bylaws Art. III §19.A.1), but a majority of all owners can waive it each year, and the Board decides when a cost is "unexpected" and when reserves are "insufficient." The 1991 vote could be re-called with a lower quorum (§5.5). Nothing in the draft caps the amount.

  5. 5

    Rewrite the Covenants without an owner vote

    Covenants §9.4 (1991 §9.4)

    The Board may change the recorded Covenants to fix errors, resolve "any ambiguity," make "nominal changes," clarify "its original intent," or meet a lender's requirements, as long as the change does not "materially and negatively" affect an owner. The 1991 version had the same list but belonged to the developer, not the Board. The Board decides what is ambiguous and what is material, records the change first, and owes no advance notice.

    What you'll hear

    "It's a standard clerical clause, limited to changes that don't materially hurt anyone, and lenders require it."

    What the documents say

    The limiter is real and should be quoted. What is missing is who judges it: the Board judges materiality itself, records the amendment before owners see it, and the drafts require no notice. The lender ground is new and is the one legitimate reason for the clause; the "ambiguity" and "original intent" grounds are what allow meaning to change.

  6. 6

    Act against an owner with no hearing, anywhere

    Covenants §2.1C, §7.1D, §7.1G.3, §7.1Q.A, §9.1D; Bylaws Art. III §5, §19.B.15

    The only "opportunity to be heard" in either draft is for a director the owners are trying to remove (Bylaws Art. III §5). There is no hearing for a fine (beyond what Ohio law requires), for suspension of your vote or pool use, for pet removal, for entry onto your lot, for an order to remove decorations, for an architectural denial, or for a rental-hardship denial. The 1991 Bylaws had a hearing clause (Art. VII §1(b)).

    What you'll hear

    "Ohio law guarantees notice and a hearing before any fine."

    What the documents say

    It does, for fines and damage charges only (R.C. 5312.11(C)-(D)), and you must request the hearing within 10 days or lose it. Nothing in Ohio law or the drafts gives a hearing for suspensions, pet removal, entry, decoration orders, architectural denials, or hardship denials.

  7. 7

    No way for owners to overturn a Board rule, fine, or decision

    Bylaws Art. III §1, Art. II §12

    "Except as otherwise provided by law, the Articles, the Declaration, or these By-Laws, all of the authority of the Association shall be exercised by or under the direction of the Board of Directors." Nothing in those documents lets owners reverse a rule, a fine, a Handbook change, or a denial. The owners' only tools are amending the documents or removing directors, and each takes more than half of all lots.

    What you'll hear

    "Owners can always remove the Board."

    What the documents say

    Removing a director takes more than half of all lots (§12), not half of those voting, and an owner the Board has suspended for a "violation" cannot vote on it (§19.B.15). The 1991 By-Laws let a majority of members remove a trustee.

  8. 8

    Borrow for any purpose and pledge your future dues as collateral, without a vote

    Covenants §2.1E; Bylaws Art. III §19.B.14 (1991 §3.1(a))

    1991 let the Association borrow only to improve the common areas. The draft allows borrowing for any purpose and lets the Board pledge future assessments as security for the loan. Ohio law (R.C. 5312.09(A)(1)) requires a 75% owner vote to pledge future dues unless the Covenants specifically allow it. §2.1E is that allowance.

    What you'll hear

    "Every association needs to be able to finance a big repair."

    What the documents say

    Ohio law already allows it with a 75% owner vote (R.C. 5312.09(A)(1)). Voting yes on §2.1E is the vote that removes the 75% requirement for every future loan.

  9. 9

    Drop the annual audit

    Bylaws Art. VII §6 (1991 Art. VIII §8(d))

    The guaranteed annual audit by a public accountant becomes a "review" done however the Board decides. Owners can force an audit only by a 75% vote. The draft does not say whether that is 75% of all owners or of those at the meeting; if all owners, it is out of reach in practice.

    What you'll hear

    "Audits are expensive; a review is standard for an association this size."

    What the documents say

    The 1991 Bylaws required an audit every year (Art. VIII §8(d)). If cost is the reason, the draft could set a dollar threshold or a fixed cycle. It instead removes the audit and sets an owner threshold the draft does not define.

  10. 10

    Decide which records a director may see

    Bylaws Art. VII §3.E

    A Board majority decides whether a director's request to see the books is for a "proper purpose." A dissenting director can be cut off from the records needed to do the job. The same draft says the Treasurer must hold the books "open for inspection and examination by the Board of Directors" (Art. IV §2.D), which contradicts it.

    What you'll hear

    "This prevents a rogue director from misusing confidential records."

    What the documents say

    Directors carry legal duties of care (R.C. 1702.30), and the draft's own Art. IV §2.D says the books are open "for inspection and examination by the Board of Directors and Lot Owners." A rule that lets the majority decide what the minority may see turns a fiduciary duty into a privilege.

  11. 11

    Narrow owners' access to records

    Bylaws Art. VII §3.C (1991 Art. XI §1)

    Enforcement files against other owners, attorney communications, contracts under negotiation, and anything older than five years are off limits without Board approval. In 1991 all records were open to any member. The new limits track Ohio's HOA law (R.C. 5312.07), which sets a floor, not a ceiling.

  12. 12

    Remove elected directors by Board vote, for more reasons

    Bylaws Art. III §5 (1991 Art. VII §1(d))

    In 1991 the Board could declare a seat vacant only for three consecutive unexcused absences. The draft drops the "excused" safeguard and adds three grounds: 30 days behind on dues, "under any existing violation" of any rule, or refusing to serve on a committee. No hearing. Owner removal of a director now requires more than half of all lots; Board removal requires only a majority of the Board.

  13. 13

    Enter your property on a Board vote

    Covenants §9.1D

    The Board may vote to send someone onto your lot to remove or correct a "violation." No notice to the owner is required. Ohio's HOA law (R.C. 5312.06(D)(13)) allows board-authorized entry only when there is "an imminent risk of damage or harm"; this clause goes further and depends on owner approval of the Covenants.

  14. 14

    Bill you the Association's legal fees, with or without a case

    Covenants §9.1

    Enforcement costs and attorney fees are owed by the "Owner in violation" whether or not any case is filed. Ohio law (R.C. 5312.11(A)(3)) does allow an association to assess enforcement costs. Your protection is a court action to remove an improper lien, in which the court may award you attorney fees (R.C. 5312.12(D)); the drafts do not mention it.

  15. 15

    Sole discretion over who may rent

    Covenants §7.1Q.A, §7.1Q.J

    An open-ended list of examples with no defined standard, no appeal, one-year approvals, and any transaction that does not comply is "void" unless the Board approves it. Grandfathered owners forfeit the right to rent for being 10 days late; the draft has no way to get it back.

  16. 16

    Cancel a non-resident owner's vote

    Covenants §3.2

    An owner who does not live in the home, and whose lapse in contact information keeps the Association from reaching them with required notices, is "deemed" to have given up the right to vote on everything. The only vote suspension Ohio's HOA law lists is for being more than 30 days behind on dues (R.C. 5312.06(D)(15)).

  17. 17

    Charge pool fees and suspend pool use on a Board "finding"

    Covenants §2.1C-D; Bylaws Art. III §19.B.9

    The Board may charge admission or use fees for the pool and other facilities on top of dues, and may suspend an owner's, tenant's, or occupant's use on its own finding of damage or "threats." No hearing, no time limit. In 1991 pool use came with the lot and had no fee power.

  18. 18

    Order a pet removed, no hearing

    Covenants §7.1D

    The Board's "reasonable judgment" that a pet is a nuisance leads to permanent removal on 10 days' notice.

  19. 19

    Open-ended "good taste" and "sole discretion" standards

    Covenants §7.1G.3, §7.1H

    Decorations the Association "deems in its discretion" to be lewd, horrific, shocking, discriminatory, vulgar, or disturbing "to a reasonable average person" must come down; permanent lighting is approved at "sole discretion." No hearing.

  20. 20

    Any turnout is a quorum, and the presiding officer sets the agenda

    Bylaws Art. II §6, §10 (1991 Art. III §4, §8)

Where the draft puts an owner in a dispute

  1. 1

    The clock runs against you, never against the Board.

    You must request a fine hearing within 10 days or lose it (R.C. 5312.11(D)(1)). A lien can be filed 10 days after any assessment is late (§4.6A). A grandfathered landlord loses the right to rent at 10 days late (§7.1Q.B.3). A pet must be gone in 10 days (§7.1D). The Association has no deadline to answer your architectural request (§6.1). The only deadline that runs in your favor in a dispute is the Board's 30 days to send you the written result after a hearing.

  2. 2

    The Board acts first; you react.

    Suspension of your vote takes effect when the Board decides you are "in violation" (Bylaws Art. III §19.B.15). Entry onto your lot follows a Board vote, with no notice (§9.1D). A §9.4 amendment is recorded before you hear of it. There is no step at which the owner is heard before any of these, so the owner's first opportunity to contest is after the fact.

  3. 3

    Pay first, or carry a lien.

    A fine and the cost of "abating" a violation are Individual Lot Assessments (§4.4), and the Association's attorney fees are added to what you owe (§9.1, §4.6). Unpaid for 10 days, they are a lien on your home (§4.6), which can be foreclosed. To contest an improper lien you go to court (R.C. 5312.12(D)). The practical choice is pay and then sue to recover, or refuse and litigate with a lien on the house.

  4. 4

    You fund both sides.

    The Association's lawyer is already retained and paid from assessments; its litigation costs are Common Expenses (§1.8, §4.2B), so your dues carry your share whether you win or lose. Your own lawyer is your cost. If the Board decides you are "in violation," §9.1 adds the Association's fees to your account, lien included. No clause in the drafts makes the Association pay your fees when you prevail; the improper-lien remedy in R.C. 5312.12(D) lets a court award them, and the leasing clause (§7.1Q.H) awards fees to whoever brings a successful action.

  5. 5

    The political route is closed while you are in a dispute.

    An owner the Board has suspended cannot vote (Bylaws Art. III §19.B.15), including on removing the directors who suspended them. Removal takes more than half of all lots (Art. II §12). Any turnout is a quorum (Art. II §6), so the Board can act at a lightly attended meeting; owners cannot remove it at one.

  6. 6

    The words are the Board's to define.

    "Violent felony," "group home," "commercial foster home," "Undue Hardship," "similar nature," "unexpected," "material": none is defined. ("Good taste" is defined, as nothing a reasonable average person would find lewd, horrific, shocking, discriminatory, vulgar, or disturbing; the Association decides in its discretion whether a display meets that definition, with no hearing.) The Board applies each undefined term, and §9.4 lets the Board record an amendment resolving "any ambiguity" in favor of its own reading.

  7. 7

    What 1991 had is exactly what kept disputes out of court.

    The hearing before suspension and the 60-day cap (Bylaws Art. VII §1(b)); the 30-day deemed approval (§6.1); the two-thirds vote before any special assessment (§5.4); the annual audit and the accounting to owners (Art. VIII §8(d), Art. VII §2(a)); all books open to any member (Art. XI §1); attorney fees owed only for collecting unpaid dues (§5.1); and rules for your lot that only an owner vote could change. Each operated before a dispute existed. The draft removes the hearing, the cap, the deemed approval, the audit, and the accounting duty, narrows the records, extends fee-shifting to every violation, and moves the lot rules to the Handbook. It keeps owner removal of directors (Art. III §5) and the two-thirds vote for new capital projects (§4.3B).

Bad for homeowners

Put together: for anything other than a fine, the draft leaves an owner one external check, a court, reached on the Board's timetable, with a lien on the home in the meantime, paying their own counsel and a share of the Association's, and, if they lose, all of the Association's.

Your money: dues, fees, fines, and liens

Everything that can show up on your Association account, and what happens if you don't pay.

Raising the annual dues

Covenants §4.2A (1991 §5.3)
Today

The Board can raise dues up to 10% a year on its own. A bigger increase needs a two-thirds vote of the owners who vote at a meeting.

Proposed

Same 10% limit for the Board. A bigger increase needs "a majority of the Owners in person or by proxy at a duly called meeting."

What it means for you

The draft does not say whether that is a majority of the owners at the meeting or of all owners. If it means those at the meeting, with no minimum turnout, large increases become much easier to pass. If it means all owners, they become harder. The draft should say which.

Special assessments (one-time charges) Bad for homeowners

Covenants §4.3 (1991 §5.4-5.5)
Today

Any special assessment for a capital project needs a two-thirds vote of owners at a meeting called with 30 to 60 days' notice.

Proposed

The Board can charge a special assessment on its own, with no owner vote, to repair or replace anything the Association already owns, including "reasonable upgrades," when the cost is unexpected and reserves are short. Only brand-new capital projects or major alterations still need owner approval, and that approval rises to written consent of two-thirds of all lot owners.

What it means for you

For the pool, entrance walls, fences, and similar existing items, the Board decides and bills you. The owner vote is reserved for new projects, where it gets harder.

Bad for homeowners

Removes the owner vote on the most common kind of special assessment. The Board decides what counts as unexpected.

Fines and other individual charges Bad for homeowners

Covenants §4.1, §4.4 (1991 §5.1, §8.4)
Today

Two kinds of charges: annual dues and special assessments. The Association could also do maintenance you failed to do, after notice, and bill you.

Proposed

Adds "Individual Lot Assessments" that can be billed to one owner: fines for rule violations (including violations by your family, guests, contractors, or tenants), the cost of work the Association does on your lot after you fail to do it, the cost of cleaning up a violation, extra insurance premiums caused by you, and damage to common areas. Also adds a street-light charge for lots in a county street-light district.

What it means for you

Fines go on your account and, if unpaid, become a lien on your home like unpaid dues. Ohio law (R.C. 5312.11) requires written notice, a deadline to fix the problem where one applies, and the right to a hearing before any fine. You must request the hearing within 10 days of the notice or you lose it. The Bylaws cite that law (Art. III §19.B.10) but the Covenants do not spell out those steps.

Bad for homeowners

New fines that attach to your home. The hearing right and its 10-day request window come from Ohio law, not from the drafts.

Late payments and liens

Covenants §4.6 (1991 §5.1, §5.9, §5.10)
Today

If a payment is 30 days late, interest runs from the original due date. The Association's lien comes behind any first mortgage, and a bank that forecloses owes at most six months of back dues.

Proposed

A lien can be filed 10 days after a payment is late. It includes interest, late fees, fines, collection costs, and attorney and paralegal fees. It comes ahead of anything recorded after it except taxes and an earlier first mortgage. The Association can foreclose (force a sale of the home to collect), ask a court to collect rent from a tenant, and bid on your home at the sale. Under the new Bylaws, a payment 30 days late lets the Board demand the whole year's dues at once.

What it means for you

Falling behind gets expensive faster. Most of this tracks what Ohio's HOA law (R.C. 5312.12) now allows. If a lien is filed improperly, Ohio law lets you go to court to remove it and recover your attorney fees (R.C. 5312.12(D)); the draft does not mention that.

Losing your vote and pool access Bad for homeowners

Bylaws Art. III §19.B.15 (1991 Art. III §9, Art. VII §1(b))
Today

You lose your vote and pool use when more than 60 days behind on dues. Suspension for a rule violation requires notice and a hearing and lasts at most 60 days.

Proposed

You lose your vote and pool access when more than 30 days behind, or whenever the Board decides you are "in violation" of any rule. No hearing and no time limit are mentioned.

What it means for you

An unresolved dispute with the Board over, say, a fence could cost you your vote at the annual meeting. Ohio's HOA law lists suspension only for being more than 30 days behind on dues (R.C. 5312.06(D)(15)).

Bad for homeowners

Removes the 1991 hearing and 60-day limit, and adds a ground for suspension that Ohio's HOA law does not list.

Pool and facility fees Bad for homeowners

Covenants §2.1C-D; Bylaws Art. III §19.B.9 (1991 §3.1)
Today

Use of the pool and common areas comes with your lot. No fee power.

Proposed

The Board may charge "reasonable admission or other fees" for the pool and other facilities, and may suspend an owner's, occupant's, or tenant's use on the Board's finding of damage or "threats."

What it means for you

Pool access could carry a charge on top of dues, and could be cut off without a hearing.

Bad for homeowners

A new charge and a new suspension power, neither with a hearing or a time limit.

Reserves, borrowing, and the budget Bad for homeowners

Covenants §2.1E; Bylaws Art. III §19, Art. VI (1991 §3.1(a); Bylaws Art. VII §2(c))
Today

The Board sets dues 30 days before the year starts and mails notice 15 days ahead. The Association may borrow to improve the common areas.

Proposed

The budget must fund reserves (money set aside for future replacements) unless a majority of all owners votes each year to skip it. The Board delivers a budget summary by December 15. If no budget is adopted, last year's dues continue. The Board can borrow for any purpose and pledge future dues as collateral, with no owner vote.

What it means for you

Part of your dues goes into reserves. The Board could take out a loan that your future dues repay, without asking owners. Ohio law would require a 75% owner vote to pledge future dues unless the Covenants specifically allow it (R.C. 5312.09(A)(1)); §2.1E is that allowance.

Bad for homeowners

The Board could borrow for any purpose and pledge your future dues, with no owner vote; 1991 allowed borrowing only to improve the common areas. Approving §2.1E also removes the 75% owner vote Ohio law otherwise requires before future dues are pledged (R.C. 5312.09(A)(1)).

Audits Bad for homeowners

Bylaws Art. VII §6 (1991 Art. VIII §8(d))
Today

The Treasurer must have the books audited every year by a public accountant.

Proposed

An annual "review" done however the Board decides. A full audit happens only if owners vote for one by 75%.

What it means for you

Owners lose the guaranteed annual audit. The draft does not say whether 75% means all owners or those at the meeting.

Bad for homeowners

Removes a yearly independent check on the money.

Paying the Association's legal bills Bad for homeowners

Covenants §9.1 (1991 §9.1)
Today

Attorney fees were owed only when the Association collected unpaid dues (§5.1). The Association, the developer, or any owner could sue to enforce the rules.

Proposed

"All costs incurred in the enforcement of this Declaration, including court costs and attorney's fees, shall be paid by the Owner in violation, regardless of whether the enforcement action involves filing a complaint" with a court. §9.1 lists only the Association as enforcer; an owner's right to sue survives in §7.1Q.H and in Ohio law (R.C. 5312.13).

What it means for you

If the Association's lawyer sends you a letter about a violation, the draft says you pay for the letter, whether or not you ever go to court. Ohio law (R.C. 5312.11(A)(3)) does allow enforcement costs to be assessed to an owner. Your recourse is the court action to remove an improper lien (R.C. 5312.12(D)).

Bad for homeowners

A fee obligation with no finding that a violation occurred. Your remedy exists in Ohio law but not in the draft.

Your house and yard

What needs written approval Bad for homeowners

Covenants §6.1 (1991 §6.1)
Today

Buildings, fences, walls, structures, and exterior additions or changes need written approval from the Board. If the Board doesn't answer within 30 days, the request is automatically approved.

Proposed

Any "Exterior Modification" that noticeably affects appearance needs written approval first from "the Association or its designated committee." The list is "broadly construed" and includes grading, decks, patios, play structures, sheds, pools, hot tubs, colors, materials, noticeable landscaping or tree changes, resurfacing a driveway, awnings, fences, retaining walls, exterior lighting (including bulb wattage), antennas and satellite dishes, and mailboxes. What it means for you

Existing fences, sheds, lights, and dishes Bad for homeowners

Covenants §6.1, §6.2 (1991 §6.1, §6.2.11)
Today

The 1991 rules limited fences to three-rail split rail or a hedge, up to 4 feet, behind the rear of the house, unless the Board approved otherwise, and required plan approval, with automatic approval after 30 days. Proposed

All of those are approval items with standards in the Handbook, and the Covenants now adopt those standards. The draft contains no sentence protecting improvements that were legal when installed.

What it means for you

Whether your existing fence, shed, or lighting is "approved" is not addressed.

Bad for homeowners

No grandfather clause (a rule that lets existing things continue under the old rules) for improvements that complied with the 1991 Covenants.

No notice to the owner is mentioned. ("Living Unit" is not a defined term in the draft.)

What it means for you

The Board can vote to send someone onto your property to remove a violation. Ohio's HOA law lists board-authorized entry only for conditions involving "an imminent risk of damage or harm" (R.C. 5312.06(D)(13)).

Bad for homeowners

Entry onto your lot without notice or hearing, beyond the emergency entry Ohio's HOA law lists.

Changing the Covenants without an owner vote Bad for homeowners

Covenants §9.4 (1991 §9.4)
Today

The developer could make corrections without a vote: typos, ambiguities, nominal changes, clarifying its original intent. That power ended with the developer.

Proposed

The Board may amend the Covenants without an owner vote for the same four reasons, plus changes a lender requires, as long as the change does not "materially and negatively" affect an owner's interest or rights.

What it means for you

The power moves from the developer to the Board. The Board decides what is ambiguous, what its original intent was, and what is material. It records the change first and owes owners no advance notice. Ohio law leaves the amendment method to the Covenants (R.C. 5312.05(A)), so approving this clause is what creates the power.

Bad for homeowners

Lets the Board change the recorded rules with no owner vote and no advance notice, within a limit ("materially and negatively") the Board itself judges.

Today

Not addressed.

Proposed

Board meetings are generally open to owners to observe, under rules the Board sets for comments. The Board may go into closed session for personnel, legal, or "similar" matters after announcing the topic.

What it means for you

Confirms owners can watch; the Board decides what is "similar."

The Board's report to owners Bad for homeowners

1991 Bylaws Art. VII §2(a), Art. VIII §8(d)
Today

The Board had to present a complete record of its acts and affairs at the annual meeting, or at a special meeting when 30% of owners demanded it in writing. The Treasurer had to deliver the budget and an income statement to every member.

Proposed

The Board delivers a budget summary by December 15 and the Treasurer presents "abstracts" at meetings. The duty to account and the 30% demand right are not carried over.

What it means for you

Bad for homeowners

Removes the Board's 1991 duty to account to owners.

Officers and the management company

Bylaws Art. IV (1991 Art. VIII)
Today

The President and Vice President must be trustees. Only Secretary and Treasurer can be the same person.

Proposed

The draft drops the sentence requiring the President and Vice President to be directors, but Ohio's HOA law still requires all officers to be elected from the Board (R.C. 5312.04(A)). Any offices can be combined except President and Vice President. Every officer duty can be delegated to a hired management company. Every director must serve on at least one committee.

What it means for you

Day-to-day operations can be run by a management company under the Board's direction.

Your right to see Association records Bad for homeowners

Bylaws Art. VII §3 (1991 Art. XI §1)
Today

All books, records, and papers open to any member during business hours.

Proposed

Follows Ohio law (R.C. 5312.07): you may see financial records, dues collection records, minutes, and the owner list, under reasonable rules and copying fees. Narrows owner access and lets the Board majority control a director's access, contradicting Art. IV §2.D.

Renting your home

The single largest change to what you can do with your property, by number of owners it can affect.

The rental ban Bad for homeowners

Covenants §7.1Q
Today

No restriction. Any owner may rent.

Proposed

"No part of any Lot, including the home thereon, may be leased unless the Owner can demonstrate an 'Undue Hardship.'"

What it means for you

You cannot rent your home unless the Board grants an exception, or you qualify as a grandfathered owner, or the occupant is an immediate family member, or the owner is a first mortgagee that took the home (§7.1Q.A, B, K, L).

Bad for homeowners

Takes away a use of your property that you have today.

The hardship exception Bad for homeowners

Covenants §7.1Q.A
Today

Not applicable.

Proposed

The Board has "sole discretion" to decide whether you have an undue hardship. Examples: a job transfer 100 or more miles away after your home fails to sell in six months at appraised value; your estate after you die; a home listed a full year at appraised value; admission to a nursing home. An exception lasts only 12 months, then you must reapply.

What it means for you

The examples are open-ended ("include but are not limited to"), nothing defines what qualifies, there is no appeal, and the answer can change each year.

Bad for homeowners

A 12-month exception paired with a required 12-month minimum lease (below) means every hardship landlord must reapply while a lease is running. If the renewal is denied, the owner cannot comply, because holdover tenancies (a tenant staying on after the lease ends) are also prohibited.

Owners who rent today (grandfathering) Bad for homeowners

Covenants §7.1Q.B
Today

Not applicable.

Proposed

If you are renting when the new rules take effect, you may keep renting until you sell or transfer the home. To qualify you must, within 30 days: notify the Association in writing, provide a copy of the lease, and provide contact information for every occupant and for yourself. You forfeit grandfathered status if you are ever 10 days late on dues; the draft has no way to get it back.

What it means for you

Current landlords have 30 days to file. Missing the deadline, or one late payment, ends the right to rent.

Bad for homeowners

Loss of the right to rent for a 10-day late payment, with no way back in the draft.

Short-term rentals

Covenants §7.1Q.C
Today

Not addressed.

Proposed

No rentals under 30 days, no hotel-style services, no renting rooms to "roomers or boarders" who are neither related to nor employed by the owner. Airbnb, Vrbo, and similar are named and banned.

What it means for you

Short-term rentals end for everyone, including grandfathered owners, because the grandfather clause only preserves leases of 30 days or more.

Lease rules

Covenants §7.1Q.D-G
Today

Not addressed.

Proposed

Leases must be written, at least 12 months, and renewed for at least 12 months. Every proposed lease must be given to the Association before signing (rent and tenant personal details may be blacked out) and a signed copy afterward. The Association may require the names of everyone who will live there.

What it means for you

Paperwork with the Association becomes part of renting.

The Association can evict your tenant

Covenants §7.1Q.I-J
Today

Not addressed.

Proposed

The Association may file an eviction against your tenant, in your name, for violating the rules, after 10 days' notice to you. You pay the legal costs as a charge on your lot. Any lease that doesn't follow these rules is "void" unless the Board approves it.

What it means for you

A rule violation by a tenant can turn into an eviction you pay for.

Family members and banks

Covenants §7.1Q.K-L
Today

Not applicable.

Proposed

Letting your parent, sibling, or child live in the home is not "renting." A first mortgagee (usually the bank) that becomes the owner may rent it.

What it means for you

Grandparents, grandchildren, and in-laws are not on the family list.

Who can live in your home

New limits on occupants, plus a new permission for home businesses.

Violent felons and sex offenders

Covenants §7.1P
Today

No restriction.

Proposed

"Anyone who has been convicted of a violent felony in any state or country or who is a registered sex offender for a Tier 2 or Tier 3 sexual offense in any state or country shall not occupy any Lot." The draft adds that the Association has no duty to check anyone's record.

What it means for you

The ban has no time limit and applies to owners living in their own homes as well as tenants. "Violent felony" is not defined in the draft. Tier 2 and Tier 3 are registry levels used by Ohio and some other states; other states and countries classify offenses differently. The draft does not say whether it applies to people already living in Polo Fields, and it says the Association has no duty to check anyone's record; nothing in the draft says who does. Federal fair-housing law does not list criminal history as a protected class; HUD guidance from 2016 on criminal-record screening was withdrawn in 2025.

"Single-family" redefined: the three-unrelated-people rule Bad for homeowners

Covenants §7.1A (1991 §7.1.1)
Today

Homes must be used for "residential purposes." No definition.

Proposed

A home may be occupied by no more than three people who are not related by blood, marriage, or legal adoption. No boarding houses, group homes, "commercial foster homes," fraternity or sorority houses, or "similar type of lodging, care, or treatment facility."

What it means for you

Two unmarried adults with two roommates could be in violation. So could a family with more than three foster children, since foster children are not related by blood, marriage, or adoption, or a licensed foster home the Board decides is "commercial" (Ohio has no such license category). A small group home for people with disabilities is banned outright. Federal fair-housing law protects people with disabilities (42 U.S.C. 3604(f)) and families with children, including children in a household's legal custody (42 U.S.C. 3602(k)). In City of Edmonds v. Oxford House (1995) the U.S. Supreme Court held that a rule defining "family" by relationship is not an exempt "maximum occupancy" limit under that law, so the law applies to it.

Bad for homeowners

Limits who can live in your home. As applied to group homes for people with disabilities and to foster families, it is the kind of rule federal fair-housing law addresses (Edmonds v. Oxford House, 1995; 42 U.S.C. 3604(f), 3602(k)).

Fair-housing rights the drafts don't mention (no change from 1991)

Covenants §6.1, §7.1A, §7.1D, §7.1M, §7.1Q; Bylaws Art. VII §2
Today

Not addressed in 1991 either.

Proposed

Neither draft contains a reasonable-accommodation clause or any reference to fair-housing law. The Bylaws' conflicts clause lists Ohio law, the Declaration, the Articles, and the Bylaws, and not federal law.

What it means for you

Federal law (42 U.S.C. 3604(f)(3)) requires the Association to make reasonable exceptions to its rules for a person with a disability: a ramp or lift under §6.1, an assistance animal under §7.1D, a live-in aide under §7.1A or §7.1Q.C, an accessible van under §7.1M. That right exists whether or not the documents say so. The drafts do not tell owners it exists, and Handbook standards for dishes, solar, and flags are limited by federal and Ohio law no matter what the Handbook says.

Signs, flags, decorations, and lights

What you can display, and who decides what is in good taste.

Signs Good for homeowners

Covenants §7.1E (1991 §7.1.4)
Today

No signs except one for-sale sign up to 4 square feet.

Proposed

One for-sale or for-rent sign, two security-system signs, two beware-of-dog signs, and "Seasonal, Holiday, and Yard Décor." Nothing else.

What it means for you

This is an expansion. Today the only sign the Covenants allow is one for-sale sign; the draft adds a for-rent sign, security signs, dog signs, and seasonal, holiday, and occasion signs (a "congratulations graduate" sign counts as décor). What does not change: political, candidate, school-levy, and cause signs are banned today and stay banned, and religious signs are allowed only when tied to a nationally recognized holiday. The First Amendment applies to government, not to a private association, and Ohio has no law protecting political yard signs from HOA rules (a bill to add one, H.B. 16 of the 136th General Assembly, was last heard in committee in April 2025).

Good for homeowners

More kinds of signs are allowed than under the 1991 rule, which permitted one for-sale sign and nothing else. The ban on political and religious signs is not new.

Flags Bad for homeowners

Covenants §7.1F
Today

No flag rule. The 1991 Covenants do not mention flags, so any flag may be flown, subject only to the general sign and approval rules.

Proposed

New rule. Flags are prohibited except: the U.S. flag (official design only, displayed "according to official federal protocols"), the Ohio flag, the POW/MIA flag, "U.S. Armed Services flags," and decorative seasonal, holiday, school, or sports-team flags. Altered U.S. flags (colored stripes, an image overlaid) are named and banned. A flagpole needs approval, and the Association has "reasonable discretion to determine the size, placement, and finish."

What it means for you

A rule where there was none. The U.S., Ohio, POW/MIA, armed-services, seasonal, school, and sports-team flags are expressly allowed; political, cause, religious, and altered-U.S. flags become prohibited; and a flagpole needs approval. Ohio's flag law (R.C. 5301.072) protects the U.S. flag, the Ohio flag, the POW/MIA flag, and a blue-star or gold-star service banner in a window; it does not protect Army, Navy, or other branch flags, so those are allowed by the draft, not by law. The Ohio statute protects a U.S. flag displayed either under federal flag customs or with the property owner's consent; the draft lists the federal customs and omits the owner-consent option. The statute also lets an association require only that the flag and flagpole be an appropriate size for the surrounding homes. Federal law (the Freedom to Display the American Flag Act of 2005) allows reasonable time, place, and manner rules for the U.S. flag.

Bad for homeowners

New restriction: flags you may fly today (political, cause, religious) become prohibited, and a flagpole needs approval. The U.S.-flag rule also omits the owner-consent option in R.C. 5301.072, and the flagpole clause claims discretion over placement and finish where the statute mentions only size.

Seasonal and holiday decorations Good for homeowners

Covenants §7.1G (1991 §7.1.9)
Today

Only Christmas lights are addressed: up no sooner than four weeks before, down within four weeks after.

Proposed

Decorations, wreaths, lights (including projections), yard signs, and flags for any season, nationally recognized holiday, school, or sports team are allowed, plus temporary signs for birthdays, graduations, and similar occasions. Lights for any holiday may go up six weeks before and must come down four weeks after, weather permitting. The Board may set rules on number, timing, and placement. Everything must be in "good taste": nothing "a reasonable average person would consider lewd, horrific, shocking, discriminatory, vulgar, or disturbing," and the Association may order removal of anything "it deems in its discretion" to fit that description.

What it means for you

Most of what people already put up becomes officially allowed. Halloween displays and anything at the edge are subject to the Association's judgment, with no hearing.

Good for homeowners

Seasonal, holiday, school, sports, and occasion displays are expressly permitted, and holiday lights get two extra weeks.

Permanent outdoor lighting

Covenants §7.1H
Today

A wattage limit on mercury-vapor lights; otherwise covered by the general approval rule for exterior changes.

Any permanent exterior lighting, on the house or in landscaping, needs prior approval at the Association's sole discretion. Permanently installed holiday-style lighting must follow the seasonal rules.

Glossary

Covenants / Declaration. The recorded rules attached to every lot in Polo Fields. Changing them normally takes an owner vote.
Bylaws. The rules for running the Association: meetings, elections, the Board, officers.
ARC (Architectural Review Committee). The committee that reviews requests to change the outside of a home. The Handbook says the Board appoints it and it is made up of the Board plus owners the Board selects at the annual meeting, one per household. It reviews plans and can propose Handbook changes, which the Board approves.
Handbook / Rules / Design Guidelines. Three names the drafts use for the Board's written standards. The Handbook exists today. It is not recorded, owners do not vote on it, and the ARC and Board change it and publish the result. Under the drafts, breaking a Handbook rule is a Covenant violation, with fines, liens, entry, and attorney fees behind it.
Trustee / Director. The 1991 documents call Board members trustees; the drafts call them directors. Same role.
Assessment. Any charge the Association bills you: annual dues, a special assessment, or an individual charge such as a fine.
Special assessment. A one-time charge on top of dues, usually for a large repair or project.
Fine. The drafts call it an "Individual Lot Assessment" or "enforcement assessment." Ohio law requires notice and a hearing before one is charged.
Lien. A legal claim on your home for money owed. It must be paid before you can sell with clear title and can be foreclosed.
Foreclose. Force a sale of the home to collect a debt secured by a lien.
Quorum. The minimum number of owners who must be present for a meeting to take action.
Proxy. Written permission for someone else to cast your vote at a meeting.
Grandfathered. Allowed to continue under the old rules after new rules take effect.
Easement. A right for someone else (the Association, a utility) to use part of your lot for a specific purpose.
Variance. Permission from the Board to do something the rules would otherwise not allow.
Reserves. Money set aside each year to replace big-ticket items later, so a special assessment is not needed.
Reasonable accommodation. An exception to a rule that federal fair-housing law requires for a person with a disability.
R.C. 5312. Ohio's Planned Community Law, the state statute that governs homeowners associations like Polo Fields. R.C. 1702 is Ohio's nonprofit corporation law, which also applies.
